# Product lens
Score: 5 | Vote: FUND IF

## What is strong
- The moment of use is easy to picture and it matters. A caregiver is worried, opens the app, sees what the wearer sees and talks to them. The pitch fits in one sentence: "See, hear and find your loved one when they need you."
- One device and one app joining two people is a whole experience the business could own end to end, from unboxing to pairing to the first call.
- Privacy mode shows the founder has thought about the wearer as a person and not only as someone being watched. The wearer is easy to forget, and the founder hasn't.
- The features clearly hang together: video, voice, SOS and GPS all serve one job, getting help to the right person quickly.

## What would kill it (the top 3, most dangerous first)
1. **No focus.** The brief serves children with special needs, elderly people with dementia, "any loved one", and later hospitals, nursing homes and rescuers, and it ends with "and more". Each of these wearers needs a different way to attach it, different controls, a different privacy default and a different caregiver flow. A device built for everyone will feel clumsy to each of them. The Product lens would cut down to one wearer and one caregiver before anything gets built.
2. **The seams in the experience.** This product passes through more hands and systems than most: wearer to device, device to network, network to app, app to caregiver, and in time caregiver to emergency services. Battery dying, lost signal, a missed SOS, video lagging at the moment it counts, a wearer who takes it off or can't find the button. Any one of these breaks the promise, and for this buyer a single broken moment ends the trust. Nothing in the brief says who owns connectivity, or what happens when it fails.
3. **The wearer's first five minutes are undefined.** The buyer is the caregiver, but whether this works depends on someone with dementia or an intellectual disability putting it on every day, keeping it on and using SOS correctly. If it is uncomfortable, confusing or embarrassing to wear, it ends up in a drawer. "Several ways to attach it" is a list of options and doesn't yet describe a design.

## The questions I would ask the founder
- Who is the single first wearer, and who is the caregiver watching? Describe one real morning with the product.
- What does the wearer actually have to do, apart from wearing it? Can that be nothing at all?
- What happens in the 60 seconds after the SOS button is pressed? Who is alerted, by what route, and what if they don't answer?
- How long does the battery last on a day of real use, and how does the wearer charge it without help?
- How does it connect (cellular, the phone, Wi-Fi), and who pays for that data? This is a hidden part of the product and it decides the subscription.
- What would you cut from the current feature list to ship something that works perfectly?
- Who in the family installs and pairs it, and how long does that take?

Open questions this lens cannot answer: what other wearable and tracker products already offer these families (/founder-competitors); whether caregivers will pay $200–300 and which wearer group wants this most (/founder-consumer); what the device, connectivity and any subscription cost to deliver (/founder-cfo).

## If FUND IF: the conditions
1. Choose one wearer segment and one caregiver for version one. Hospitals, nursing homes and rescuers move to a later roadmap, and "and more" comes off the list.
2. Write the end-to-end experience for that segment before engineering starts: unboxing, setup, a normal day, charging, an SOS, a lost signal. Each step gets a named owner and a fallback.
3. Show that the wearer will keep it on. A small real-world test with the chosen wearers should prove they wear it daily for at least a few weeks.
4. Decide how it connects and price the subscription from that, so the caregiver's cost is clear before they buy.
