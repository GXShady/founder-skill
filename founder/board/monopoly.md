# Monopoly lens
Score: 4 | Vote: FUND IF

## What is strong
- **A plausible small market to own first.** "Families caring for a vulnerable person" is too broad, but inside it is a narrow, intense niche: families of children with intellectual disabilities or special needs. That group could be dominated before expanding to elderly care, then to hospitals, nursing homes and rescuers. That sequence is the right shape for this lens.
- **Live video plus two-way voice is the one possible step change.** Many safety devices only answer "where are they?" Seeing what the wearer sees and talking to them answers "what is happening, and are they okay?". If the product does that reliably, it is a real difference on something that matters to the buyer, not a small improvement.
- **The privacy mode hints at a secret.** An adult wearer who shares video only when they choose suggests the founder understands the dignity tension in caregiving. If the product is built around that insight, it could become a trusted brand in a category where trust decides the purchase.
- **A route to recurring revenue and expansion.** The device-plus-app model, a possible subscription, and a B2B path give the business room to build scale and a brand over time.

## What would kill it (the top 3, most dangerous first)
1. **No durable moat: it is a camera, GPS, SOS and an app.** Each part is commodity hardware. Nothing in the brief is proprietary technology, a network effect or an economy of scale. If a larger device maker or a cheaper import ships the same bundle in month six, nothing stops them. Who already sells GPS trackers, SOS pendants, kids' smartwatches or body cameras to this same buyer, and at what price, is unknown. That is the first open question (/founder-competitors).
2. **No distribution.** The market and sales channel are not decided. The buyer is a stressed caregiver who is hard to reach cheaply through ads. Without a channel such as special-needs schools, therapists, support groups or care organisations, a good product will not reach its first 100 customers. Nobody knows what each customer would cost to acquire.
3. **The breakthrough may not hold up in practice.** Live video from the body needs battery life, connectivity and data costs that work at a $200–$300 price. Wearers in the core niche may also refuse a device or remove it. If the hardware cannot deliver all day, the one 10x claim falls away. The unit cost and the ongoing data cost are unknown (/founder-cfo), and so is whether wearers will keep it on (/founder-consumer).

## The questions I would ask the founder
- Which single niche do you start with, and could you win most of it within two years?
- What do you know about this buyer that others don't, and how does that shape the product?
- Who else sells within reach of this caregiver today, and why does a family choose you over them? (/founder-competitors)
- How do your first 100 customers arrive, through which channel, and what does each cost?
- What stops a copycat with the same parts in month six: software, data, partnerships, certifications or brand?
- What does one device cost to make, and what does streaming video cost per wearer per month? (/founder-cfo)
- Will caregivers pay $200–$300 plus a subscription, and will wearers actually keep it on? (/founder-consumer)
- Why now? What has changed in hardware, connectivity or care needs that makes this possible today?

## If FUND IF: the conditions
1. Narrow the launch to one niche (for example, families of children with special needs) and show it can be dominated.
2. Name a specific, testable distribution channel to the first 100 customers, with an estimated cost per customer.
3. Build at least one moat beyond the hardware bundle: partnerships with care organisations, software that gets better with use, a trust brand, or certifications for later B2B. Map it against the real competitors (/founder-competitors).
4. Show that the core claim, reliable all-day live video plus voice, works at a unit cost and data cost that leave margin at the planned price (/founder-cfo).
